% ---------- ABSTRACT ----------
\begin{abstract}
Transformer-based models achieve state-of-the-art performance in named entity recognition, but their increasing size raises concerns regarding training and deployment efficiency.
This work investigates the trade-offs between model size, training regime, and architectural design for German named entity recognition.
We compare a distilled Transformer and two base-sized BERT variants under full fine-tuning and frozen encoder setups, and additionally evaluate a last-layer aggregation strategy based on concatenating the final four hidden layers.
All experiments are conducted on the GermEval14 benchmark using strict, entity-level evaluation on outer entities.

The results show that full fine-tuning consistently outperforms frozen encoder training across all models, with performance differences of approximately 8-12 F1 points.
While base-sized models achieve the highest absolute F1-scores when fully fine-tuned, a compact model such as DistilBERT offers a particularly favorable efficiency-performance trade-off, clearly outperforming larger models trained with frozen encoders.
Architectural extensions provide limited gains under restricted training but do not compensate for the absence of encoder-level adaptation.
Overall, the findings highlight encoder adaptability as the primary driver of performance in German named entity recognition, outweighing model size alone.
\end{abstract}


% ---------- 1. INTRO ----------
\section{Introduction}

Named entity recognition (NER) is a core task in natural language processing that enables the extraction of structured information from unstructured text \cite{tjong_kim_sang_introduction_2003}.
In recent years, Transformer-based encoders have become the dominant modeling paradigm for NER and other sequence labeling tasks due to their ability to learn rich contextual token representations \cite{vaswani_attention_2017}.
However, these performance gains are often accompanied by increased computational cost, raising practical concerns regarding training efficiency and deployment.

These concerns are particularly relevant for German NER, where evaluation relies on fine-grained label sets and strict span-based matching on benchmarks such as GermEval14 \cite{benikova_germeval_nodate}.
As a result, competitive systems commonly employ pretrained Transformer encoders that are fully fine-tuned for the downstream task.
While effective, this practice motivates the question of when smaller models or computationally cheaper training strategies can provide competitive alternatives.

Recent work has introduced several pretrained Transformer language models specifically for German, demonstrating the benefits of language-specific pretraining but also highlighting the computational footprint of modern encoders \cite{scheible_gottbert_2024}.
Despite this progress, systematic analyses that jointly examine model size, training regime, and lightweight architectural modifications for German NER - while also considering both training and inference efficiency - remain limited.

In this work, we present a controlled empirical study of these trade-offs on the GermEval14 NER benchmark \cite{benikova_germeval_nodate}.
We compare a distilled Transformer and two base-sized BERT models under full fine-tuning and frozen encoder setups, and additionally evaluate a last-layer aggregation variant based on concatenating the final four hidden layers.
By jointly analyzing predictive performance together with training runtime and inference cost, this study aims to clarify which factors primarily drive effectiveness in practical German NER systems.

% ---------- 2. PROBLEM + RESEARCH QUESTIONS ----------
\section{Problem Statement and Research Questions}

Transformer-based models achieve strong performance in named entity recognition, but their effectiveness comes at a substantial computational cost.
In practical settings, this raises the question of how model size, training regime, and lightweight architectural modifications jointly influence both predictive performance and efficiency.
In particular, it is unclear to what extent full end-to-end fine-tuning is necessary for high-quality German NER, and whether smaller models or restricted training strategies can offer competitive trade-offs.

This work addresses the following research questions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1:} How does full fine-tuning compare to a frozen encoder regime in terms of NER performance and computational efficiency for German Transformer-based models?
    \item \textbf{RQ2:} How does model size affect the performance-efficiency trade-off under different training regimes?
    \item \textbf{RQ3:} Can lightweight architectural extensions, such as concatenating representations from multiple final layers, mitigate performance degradation under restricted training?
\end{itemize}

Based on prior findings in Transformer-based NER, we hypothesize that full fine-tuning will yield the highest performance across models, while smaller architectures will exhibit more favorable efficiency--performance trade-offs. Furthermore, architectural extensions are expected to provide limited improvements under frozen encoder setups but not fully compensate for the absence of encoder adaptation.
% ---------- 3. BACKGROUND (SHORT) ----------
\section{Background}

\subsection{Transformer-based token classification}
Modern NER systems commonly formulate the task as token-level classification using contextualized representations produced by a Transformer encoder \cite{vaswani_attention_2017}.
For each input token, the encoder outputs a hidden representation that is mapped to label logits by a lightweight classification head and trained using cross-entropy loss.
Entity spans are reconstructed using a tagging scheme such as BIO \cite{tjong_kim_sang_introduction_2003}.
This setup has become the standard formulation for Transformer-based NER.

\subsection{GermEval14 in historical context}
The GermEval 2014 shared task predates the use of Transformer architectures and provides a useful reference point for historical NER performance on German.
In the original shared task, the best-performing systems based on CRFs and feature-rich statistical models achieved an overall F1-score of 77.1 under the official strict evaluation metric, and 79.8 on first-level (outer) entities, with substantially lower performance on embedded entities \cite{benikova_germeval_nodate}.
These results illustrate the difficulty of German NER prior to large-scale contextual pretraining.

\subsection{German pretrained Transformer models}
Recent years have seen the development of pretrained Transformer language models specifically for German, trained on orders of magnitude more unlabeled text than was available at the time of GermEval14.
For example, Chan et al.\ pretrain German BERT-style models on up to 12 billion tokens of German text \cite{chan_germans_2020}.
Similarly, GottBERT is a monolingual German RoBERTa-style model pretrained on approximately 145 GB of filtered German text from the OSCAR corpus \cite{scheible_gottbert_2024}.
When evaluated on GermEval14, these models report F1-scores of approximately 88.3 (GottBERT-base) and 89.2 (GottBERT-large), demonstrating the impact of large-scale pretraining on German NER performance \cite{scheible_gottbert_2024}.

% ---------- 4. RELATED WORK ----------
\section{Related Work}

Early work on named entity recognition relied on feature-based statistical models such as conditional random fields, as exemplified by the CoNLL-2003 shared task \cite{tjong_kim_sang_introduction_2003}.
With the advent of pretrained Transformer encoders, NER performance improved substantially by fine-tuning contextual representations with token classification heads \cite{kaur_bert-ner_2024}.
Most existing studies focus primarily on maximizing predictive performance and assume full end-to-end fine-tuning.

For German, several monolingual and multilingual pretrained Transformer models have been proposed, showing consistent improvements over earlier approaches and multilingual baselines \cite{chan_germans_2020,scheible_gottbert_2024}.
However, evaluations of these models typically rely on full fine-tuning and do not systematically examine alternative training regimes, such as frozen encoders, or analyze training and inference efficiency.

A smaller body of work considers efficiency-oriented modeling choices, including reduced model sizes or constrained parameter updates.
Nevertheless, for German NER in particular, there is a lack of controlled empirical studies that jointly analyze model size, training regime, and lightweight architectural modifications while explicitly reporting computational cost.
The present work addresses this gap by providing a systematic comparison of these factors on GermEval14.

\section{Dataset}
% GermEval14 format, label set, outer vs nested, what you use/ignore, preprocessing.
The dataset we use in this study is the GermEval 2014 NER dataset \cite{germeval14_hf}, which builds upon the annotation published by Benikova \cite{benikova_nosta-d_nodate}, which after some adjustments became the dataset used for the GermEval 2014 shared task \cite{benikova_germeval_nodate} and was downloaded from the Hugging Face datasets library. The dataset contains German text from Wikipedia and news articles, annotated with named entities in a nested format. In total the dataset contains over 30,000 sentences with approximately 590,000 tokens. The dataset is split into:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Training set: 24,002 sentences
    \item Development set: 2,200 sentences
    \item Test set: 5,100 sentences
\end{itemize}
The dataset includes annotations for 4 main entity types: \textit{Person}, \textit{Organization}, \textit{Location}, and \textit{Other}. Each entity type also has subtypes, namely \textit{part} and \textit{deriv}, which leads to a total of 12 distinct entity labels. The dataset also uses nested annotations, meaning that entities can be contained within other entities. An example is a sports club name that contains a location token, where the full span is annotated as an organization while a sub-span inside it is annotated as a location. This follows the more general application of transformer-based Named Entity Recognition, where other datasets often do not have the nested elements, such as the CoNLL-2003 dataset \cite{tjong_kim_sang_introduction_2003} used for example in English NER. \cite{kaur_bert-ner_2024}.

% ---------- 6. APPROACH ----------
\section{Approach}
\subsection{Models}
% Which pretrained checkpoints and why (small/base, mono/multi).
For this study, we selected three transformer-based models that vary in size and purpose. All models were downloaded from the Hugging Face model hub. The models chosen are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{bert-base-german-cased} \cite{bert_base_german_cased}: The German BERT base model is a transformer model published in 2019, pretrained on roughly 12GB of German text data, case-sensitive input, and evaluated on various tasks including CONLL03, GermEval14, and GNAD. The model has 12 layers, each with 12 attention heads, the tokens are embedded into a 768-dimensional space, with the feed-forward layers having a dimensionality of 3072. The vocabulary size of the model is 30,000 tokens, leading to a total of around 110 million parameters.
    \item \textbf{distilbert-base-german-cased} \cite{distilbert_base_german_cased}: DistilBERT is a compressed version of BERT trained through knowledge distillation, where a smaller student model is trained to match the behavior of a larger teacher model. The DistilBERT model has the same hidden dimensionality, feed-forward size, and number of attention heads as the BERT base model, but reduces the number of transformer layers from 12 to 6, resulting in a significantly smaller and faster model, while retaining the same width. The model uses a vocabulary of 31,102 tokens and contains approximately 67 million parameters.
    \item \textbf{bert-base-multilingual-cased} \cite{bert_base_multilingual_cased}: The multilingual BERT base model is a transformer model released by Google, pretrained with a masked language modeling objective on the Wikipedias of 104 languages (including German), and is case sensitive. It follows the BERT-base architecture with 12 layers and 12 attention heads, a hidden size of 768 and a feed-forward size of 3072. Due to its much larger vocabulary (119{,}547 tokens), the model has approximately 179 million parameters.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Training Strategies}
% Frozen encoder vs full fine-tuning; last4 concat variant (if used).
We use two different training strategies for fine-tuning all models on the GermEval14 dataset. In all cases, we add a token classification head as a linear layer on top of the transformer to predict the entity labels for each token. We then experiment with the following strategies for training:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Full Fine-Tuning}: In this strategy, we train the entire model, including all transformer layers and the classification head, on the GermEval14 training set. This allows the model to adapt all its parameters to the specific NER task.
    \item \textbf{Frozen Encoder}: In this strategy, we freeze all the transformer layers and only train the classification head on top. This means that the pre-trained weights of the transformer remain unchanged, and only the final layer is updated during training. This approach is computationally more efficient and requires less memory, as only the parameters of the classification head need to be stored and updated.
\end{itemize}
We also experiment with an additional variant where we concatenate the last four hidden layers of the transformer as input to the classification head, instead of just using the final layer's output. This approach needs a modification of the classification head to accommodate the increased input dimensionality, so its size is $4 * 768 = 3072$.

This choice is motivated by prior work showing that combining representations from multiple layers can improve performance on NER tasks. \cite{tamburini_solving_2024}.

The specific hyperparameter configurations for each strategy were selected based on a small grid search on the development set, as described in Section \ref{sec:experimental_setup}.

\subsection{Implementation Details}
\paragraph{Label set and preprocessing}
The label inventory is derived from the GermEval14 dataset by collecting all unique BIO tags (\texttt{ner\_tag}) across all dataset splits. A deterministic mapping between labels and numeric IDs (\texttt{label2id} / \texttt{id2label}) is created and passed to the model configuration to ensure consistent decoding and evaluation.

\paragraph{Tokenization and label alignment}
All models use their respective tokenizers via \texttt{AutoTokenizer}. Since the dataset provides word-level token sequences, tokenization is performed with \texttt{is\_split\_into\_words=True}, applying truncation and fixed-length padding up to a maximum sequence length of 128. Label alignment is implemented using the tokenizer's \texttt{word\_ids()} mapping: special tokens and padding positions (e.g., \texttt{[CLS]}, \texttt{[SEP]}) are masked with the ignore index \texttt{-100}. For words that are split into multiple subword tokens, the label is assigned only to the first subword token, while all subsequent subwords are masked with \texttt{-100}. This follows the standard Hugging Face token-classification preprocessing and ensures that loss and evaluation are computed only on one token per original word. \cite{huggingface_token_classification}


\paragraph{Model setup}
Models are instantiated using \texttt{AutoModelForTokenClassification}. Depending on the training strategy, either all model parameters are updated (full fine-tuning) or only the parameters of the token classification head are optimized while the transformer encoder is frozen.

\paragraph{Training Loop}
Training is implemented using the Hugging Face Trainer API, which provides a standardized training and evaluation loop for transformer models. Evaluation is performed at the end of each epoch on the development set. Final model performance is assessed on the test set; the evaluation metrics and protocol are described in detail in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}.

% ---------- 7. EVALUATION ----------
\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

\subsection{Metrics (GermEval14)}
Benikova et al.\cite{benikova_germeval_nodate} define four metrics for the shared task, but describe three main evaluation variants for nested NER. We summarize the three proposed metrics below.

Let $P_1$ and $G_1$ be the predicted/gold sets of outer (first-level) named entity chunks, and $P_2$ and $G_2$ the predicted/gold sets of inner (second-level) chunks. \cite{benikova_germeval_nodate}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Metric 1: Strict, Combined Evaluation (official)}.
    All markables across both levels are evaluated jointly, distinguishing all 12 labels (including \texttt{deriv} and \texttt{part} subtypes). A predicted chunk matches a gold chunk iff (i) label matches, (ii) span matches, and (iii) the level (outer vs.\ inner) matches:
    $
        P = P_1 \cup P_2,\quad G = G_1 \cup G_2,\quad
        p == g \Leftrightarrow \text{label}(p)=\text{label}(g)\wedge \text{span}(p)=\text{span}(g)\wedge \text{level}(p)=\text{level}(g).
    $
    \item \textbf{Metric 2: Loose, Combined Evaluation}.
    Same as Metric~1, but subtypes are collapsed (base label match is sufficient, e.g.\ \texttt{PER} matches \texttt{PERderiv}), while span and level must still match:
    $
        P = P_1 \cup P_2,\quad G = G_1 \cup G_2,\quad
        p == g \Leftrightarrow \text{baseLabel}(p)=\text{baseLabel}(g)\wedge \text{span}(p)=\text{span}(g)\wedge \text{level}(p)=\text{level}(g).
    $
    \item \textbf{Metric 3: Strict, Separate Evaluation}.
    Two separate strict evaluations are reported: one for outer entities ($P_1$ vs.\ $G_1$) and one for inner entities ($P_2$ vs.\ $G_2$), each using the traditional strict match definition (label + exact span). 
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What we evaluate in this study}
In our experiments we report:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Entity-level Precision, Recall, and F1-score} computed from predicted and gold chunks of the outer entities using strict matching (exact span and label). We report micro-averaged scores as the primary metric for model comparison, corresponding to Metric~3's outer evaluation and focusing on the standard flat NER task. In addition, we compute per-class and macro-averaged scores for diagnostic analysis, which are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:per-class-results}.

    \item \textbf{Training Runtime} (wall-clock) for each experiment, to quantify efficiency trade-offs between model size and training strategy.
    \item \textbf{Inference Runtime} (GPU execution) on the test set, to assess practical deployment efficiency.
\end{itemize}

Entity-level scores are computed by decoding BIO tag sequences into entity spans using standard BIO chunk extraction (as implemented by \texttt{seqeval}) and then applying strict matching against gold spans (exact label and exact span).
Inference runtime is measured on the full test set with the model in evaluation mode (\texttt{model.eval()}) under \texttt{torch.inference\_mode()} using pre-tokenized inputs (tokenization excluded). On GPU, inference runtime is measured on the tokenized test set using CUDA events with synchronization before and after the measured region (batch size 16).

\subsection{Evaluation protocol and outer-only choice}
The GermEval dataset provides two annotation layers and the shared task evaluates both levels.
However, in this study we only evaluate the outer entities and ignore the nested layer, as this would add complexity that is not central to the research questions.

Concretely, we evaluate predicted chunks against $G_1$ (outer gold entities) and do not model nor score $G_2$ (inner entities). This choice is motivated by:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Task alignment and comparability}: most widely used NER benchmarks - including the CoNLL-2003 dataset \cite{tjong_kim_sang_introduction_2003} - assume flat annotation; focusing on outer entities reduces GermEval to this standard setting.
    \item \textbf{Modeling scope}: nested prediction introduces an additional structural requirement (two-level outputs) beyond plain sequence tagging; our experiments target the trade-off between model size and fine-tuning strategy under a standard token-classification setup.
    \item \textbf{Data characteristics}: only a minority of entities are nested (about 7.8\% in the dataset statistics reported by the task paper), while inner entities show substantially lower performance in the shared task analysis, suggesting higher difficulty. \cite{benikova_germeval_nodate}
\end{itemize}

% ---------- 8. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP ----------
\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experimental_setup}

\subsection{Hardware and environment}
All experiments were run on a single workstation equipped with an NVIDIA RTX 3080 GPU. Even though better hardware is available, the experiments still give insight into the relative efficiency of the models and training strategies.

\subsection{Hyperparameter selection via small grid search}
To ensure a fair and computationally feasible comparison across all twelve model configurations (three architectures $\times$ frozen vs.\ full fine-tuning $\times$ last layer vs. last-4-concatenation), we performed a small but targeted hyperparameter search on a single reference model: bert-base-german-cased. We conducted the search separately for the two training regimes:\\
(i) \textbf{Full fine-tuning} (encoder + classifier head trainable), and\\
(ii) \textbf{Frozen encoder} (only the classifier head trainable).\\
To preserve comparability across configurations, we tuned hyperparameters only for the standard head and reused these settings for the last-4 concatenation variant.

To keep exploratory runs efficient, we trained on a reduced subset of 50\% of the training split while always evaluating on the full development set. For each candidate configuration, we trained for the specified number of epochs and selected the setting with the highest development F1-score (outer entities, strict matching as described in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}). We additionally recorded wall-clock runtime to ensure the chosen settings were not only accurate but also practically feasible.

\paragraph{Search space.}
For full fine-tuning, we tested learning rates $\{2\times 10^{-5}, 3\times 10^{-5}, 5\times 10^{-5}\}$ and epochs $\{3,4,5\}$.
For the frozen encoder regime, we tested higher learning rates - $\{3\times 10^{-4}, 5\times 10^{-4}, 1\times 10^{-3}, 2\times 10^{-3}, 3\times 10^{-3}\}$ - appropriate for training only a shallow head, and epochs in a slightly wider range - $\{5,8,10\}$ - to allow convergence under frozen representations.

\paragraph{Selected hyperparameters.}
We selected the following hyperparameters based on the grid search results:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Full fine-tuning:} learning rate $2\times 10^{-5}$, $4$ epochs.
    \item \textbf{Frozen encoder:} learning rate $2\times 10^{-3}$, $10$ epochs.
\end{itemize}
The full grid-search results are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:hyperparams} (Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams-full-list}).

These hyperparameters were then used unchanged for all models within the corresponding training regime to preserve comparability between architectures and to avoid confounding results with per-model tuning.

\subsection{Training configuration and reproducibility}
Unless stated otherwise, all experiments use a maximum sequence length of 128 with truncation and fixed-length padding, a batch size of 16, weight decay of 0.01, and a fixed random seed of 42.
Training is performed with the Hugging Face \texttt{Trainer} using AdamW optimization and evaluation on the development set after each epoch (\texttt{eval\_strategy="epoch"}).
We do not apply early stopping.
For each run, we report test-set results obtained from the final checkpoint after the configured number of epochs (i.e., we do not select a model based on development performance).

% ---------- 10. DISCUSSION ----------
\section{Discussion}

This section discusses the observed performance and efficiency differences across training regimes, model sizes, and architectural variants, with a particular focus on how these differences relate to properties of the Transformer architecture and training dynamics.

\paragraph{Effect of training regime.}
Across all evaluated models, full fine-tuning substantially outperforms the frozen encoder regime, with F1-score reductions of approximately 8-12 points when freezing the encoder. This indicates that task-specific adaptation of contextual representations is critical for high-quality German NER. When the encoder is frozen, only the classification head can adapt to the task, limiting the model’s ability to refine token-level representations and boundary decisions. As a result, even large pretrained encoders are unable to fully compensate for the lack of encoder-level adaptation.

\paragraph{Training runtime and parameter updates.}
Freezing the encoder leads to a large reduction in training runtime per epoch across all models, and in our setup even results in lower total training time despite the frozen encoder runs using more epochs (10 vs.\ 4).
Because the forward pass remains unchanged but backpropagation is limited to the classification head, encoder freezing removes most gradient computations and optimizer updates in the transformer stack.
When normalized per epoch, this yields a consistent speed-up of roughly 65\% across all three architectures.
While the relative reduction is similar, the absolute time saved per epoch is larger for the base-sized BERT models (about 129-136 seconds per epoch) than for DistilBERT (about 65 seconds per epoch), reflecting the higher computational cost of backpropagating through deeper encoders.
Between BERT (DE) and BERT (Multi), training times are slightly higher for the multilingual model, whereas inference times remain comparable due to identical encoder depth and width; vocabulary-size differences mainly affect the embedding layer and have a minor impact relative to sequence length and hidden dimensionality.



\paragraph{Inference time behavior.}
Inference times are largely determined by the forward pass through the encoder and are therefore mostly independent of the training regime. This explains why inference runtimes remain comparable between full fine-tuning and frozen encoder setups. Furthermore, the similar inference times observed for BERT (DE) and BERT (Multi) can be explained by their identical architectural depth and hidden dimensionality, resulting in comparable computational complexity per input sequence. In contrast, DistilBERT achieves substantially faster inference due to its reduced number of layers, despite sharing the same hidden size.

\paragraph{Model size, efficiency, and practical trade-offs.}
Although DistilBERT exhibits a modest performance gap compared to base-sized models under full fine-tuning, this gap remains relatively small when contrasted with the significant gains in training and inference efficiency. Notably, a fully fine-tuned DistilBERT clearly outperforms a frozen encoder setup using a larger BERT model, highlighting that adapting a smaller model end-to-end is more effective than relying on a larger but non-adaptive encoder. This suggests that, under constrained computational budgets, fine-tuning a compact Transformer may be a more effective strategy than freezing a larger architecture.

\paragraph{Impact of last-4-layer concatenation.}
Concatenating the last four hidden layers consistently improves performance under the frozen encoder regime, but does not yield any gains for base-sized models under full fine-tuning. This indicates that exposing the classifier to representations from multiple abstraction levels can partially mitigate the loss of encoder adaptation by providing richer contextual signals. However, the observed improvements remain limited and do not close the performance gap to full fine-tuning, suggesting that architectural modifications alone cannot fully replace encoder-level learning.

\paragraph{Summary.}
Overall, the results demonstrate that performance in German NER is primarily driven by encoder adaptability rather than model size alone, particularly for fine-grained entity distinctions. While larger models provide higher absolute performance when fully fine-tuned, smaller models such as DistilBERT offer a strong efficiency--performance trade-off and benefit disproportionately from end-to-end fine-tuning. Lightweight architectural extensions, such as last-4-layer concatenation, can improve robustness in restricted training settings but remain secondary to full encoder adaptation.

Derived entities consistently perform closer to base entities than part entities in both regimes.
Under full fine-tuning, derived entity F1-scores are comparable to or slightly below base entity performance across models.

While all groups experience performance degradation under encoder freezing, the relative drop is largest for part entities.

\paragraph{Model differences.}
Under full fine-tuning, base-sized models slightly outperform DistilBERT on the more challenging groups, particularly part entities, reflecting the benefit of increased model capacity.
Under the frozen encoder regime, DistilBERT achieves comparable or slightly higher grouped performance on base and derived entities than the base-sized BERT (DE) model while the BERT (Multi) lags behind, but part entities remain difficult across all architectures, especially for BERT (Multi).
Overall, the grouped error analysis reinforces the conclusion that encoder adaptability, rather than model size alone, is the primary driver of improvements for fine-grained entity distinctions.


% ---------- 12. LIMITATIONS ----------
\section{Limitations}

While this study provides a systematic comparison of model size, training regime, and architectural variants for German named entity recognition, several limitations should be acknowledged.

First, the experimental scope is restricted to a limited set of model architectures. Only three transformer models were evaluated, covering a distilled model and two base-sized BERT variants. Although these models represent commonly used architectures with differing capacity and efficiency characteristics, the findings may not directly generalize to larger models, alternative pretraining objectives, or more recent architectures. Consequently, conclusions regarding model size should be interpreted within the bounds of the selected model spectrum.

Second, the exploration of training strategies is intentionally constrained. The study compares two extreme regimes - full fine-tuning and fully frozen encoders - without considering intermediate approaches such as partial layer freezing, gradual unfreezing schedules, or layer-wise learning rate adaptations. These strategies could potentially offer improved trade-offs between performance and efficiency and remain unexplored in the present work.

Third, the training duration and architectural variants were examined only within a narrow range. Full fine-tuning experiments were limited to four epochs, and the last-layer aggregation analysis considered only a single configuration based on concatenation of the final four hidden layers. The impact of longer training, alternative epoch schedules, or varying the number of concatenated layers was not investigated. As a result, the reported effects of architectural modifications may not fully capture their potential under different optimization settings.

Fourth, evaluation is limited to a single dataset and task formulation. All experiments were conducted on the GermEval14 benchmark and focused exclusively on outer entities, excluding nested entity structures. While this enables a controlled and consistent comparison, it restricts the applicability of the results to flat German NER and does not address performance on nested entities or other domains.

Finally, the study focuses exclusively on named entity recognition. Although the observed trends provide insights into the relationship between encoder adaptability, model size, and efficiency, they may not transfer directly to other sequence labeling tasks or downstream NLP applications with different structural or semantic requirements.

Despite these limitations, the experimental design enables a controlled and transparent analysis of key trade-offs in Transformer-based German NER. The results therefore offer meaningful insights within the defined scope and provide a foundation for future work exploring broader model families, training strategies, and task settings.


% ---------- 13. CONCLUSION + FUTURE WORK ----------
\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

This work systematically examined the trade-offs between model size, training regime, and architectural design for German named entity recognition using Transformer-based models. Across all configurations, encoder adaptability emerged as the dominant factor for performance, with full fine-tuning consistently outperforming frozen encoder setups, even when using smaller models. While larger architectures achieved the highest absolute F1-scores under full fine-tuning, compact models such as DistilBERT offered a particularly favorable efficiency-performance balance, especially under constrained computational budgets. Lightweight architectural extensions, such as last-layer concatenation, were shown to partially mitigate performance degradation in restricted training settings, but remained secondary to end-to-end encoder adaptation.

Future work could extend this analysis to a broader range of model architectures and sizes, including more recent or larger pretrained Transformers. Additionally, intermediate fine-tuning strategies, such as partial freezing or gradual unfreezing, may yield improved trade-offs between efficiency and performance and warrant systematic investigation. Expanding the evaluation to nested entity recognition, additional datasets, or other sequence labeling tasks would further assess the generality of the observed trends.
